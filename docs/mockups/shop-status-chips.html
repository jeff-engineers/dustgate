The switch on a tool row becomes a status chip

Today every tool row carries a toggle switch on its right. It looks like a setting — auto vs. manual is the natural read — and it is not one. It is a <span> that mirrors t.on; the real control is the whole row. Two glyphs, one screen, and the collector card above uses the same switch shape for a control that is real.

The switch has to go, not get relabelled. A tool row has more than two states, and the interesting ones are the ones a switch cannot say: this saw is running and getting no air, because the jointer took the gate. That is the question the shop page exists to answer, and today it renders identically to “running and fine”.

The chip vocabulary

Collecting Green — air is reaching this tool. It won the routing vote and has a clear path to a running blower. At most one per system.
Waiting Orange — powered, but out-voted. The tool is drawing (or was switched on by hand) and something more recent holds the gate. The subtitle names who, because “which one do I turn off” is the next question.
Gate offline Red — it cannot collect, and that is a fault. The node owning this gate is not answering.
Not set up Orange. The gate has never been calibrated — nobody has shown it where the valve positions are. Nothing is broken; it is unfinished, and the canvas already dots an uncalibrated gate orange. Today this is a shop-wide banner; it is really per gate.
No reading Red. A smart outlet is configured and the poll is failing. Distinct from having no outlet at all — power here is unknown, not off.
No gate Orange, for the same reason — unfinished, not faulty. The machine has no port in any system, so no gate stands between it and the trunk. Today it is dumped into an orphan group with prose.
Idle Grey — nothing to say. Hollow rather than filled, so a shelf of sleeping tools stays quiet and the two rows that matter carry the colour.

Colour is the severity axis; the words are the specific. Red chips all read the same at a glance — this one is broken right now — and only need reading closely once you have decided to care; orange ones read as no air here, and it is not the hardware's fault. That is also why every chip carries text: green/orange/red alone fails for a colour-blind woodworker in a dim shop, and the dot before each label is a second, redundant cue rather than the cue.

Auto vs. manual is not a chip. It moves to the subtitle, where it already lives. The chip answers exactly one question — is air reaching this tool — and mixing a settings fact into that axis is what made the switch ambiguous in the first place.

Transients are deliberately absent. No “Opening…”, no “Closing…”. Gate travel is seconds and nobody is watching the phone during it; a chip that flickers through a state you cannot act on is noise. A gate that fails to arrive is a fault and gets a red chip.

Settled — what says “you can tap this”

The switch was the wrong control, but it was the only thing on the row that looked like a control. Removing it leaves a row that is still a button and no longer advertises it. Two answers:

A1 — the row is the button

Chip is pure state, the whole row takes the tap, and the press state plus a border lift carry the affordance. Cleanest, and it matches how the tool rows already behave. Risk: “run the bandsaw by hand” becomes an undiscoverable gesture — and hand-running a blower to clear a clog is a real errand, not an edge case.

Against the board LEDs

The phone is not the only thing in this shop showing status, or even the most-read one. Every board carries a pixel — on a node it is the entire user interface, screwed to a joist above a manifold with no screen, no buttons and no cable — and its vocabulary is already written down in firmware/utils/StatusLed.h. There is a third surface too: the light on the cyclone, wired years ago and owing nothing to any of this. A chip in your hand that disagrees with a pixel on the ceiling about what a colour means is a genuine hazard, so:

Green and red are the load-bearing pair, and all three surfaces already agree. Green means working, red means stop and deal with it — on the ceiling, on the cyclone, and in your hand. Those are the two a woodworker actually reads at a distance and they must never diverge — locking them is most of the value of this comparison. That the bin light was wired years before any of this existed and landed on the same two colours is a decent sign they are the right two.

Orange diverges, and that is defensible — but only because the UI has no transients. On the pixel, orange is motion. On a chip, orange is no air here, and it isn’t the hardware’s fault. Those look contradictory until you notice they can never describe the same moment: the pixel’s orange window is a gate in flight, which is precisely the window we decided not to render. By the time a chip has settled on orange the pixel beside it has gone back to green or red. Two different subjects — the pixel diagnoses a board, the chip diagnoses the airflow — and they are disjoint in time.

Blue and white don’t come across, deliberately. White is the captive portal: if it’s lit there is no app to look at, so it cannot collide. Blue is the honest tempting one — “configured but not ready to work” is exactly Not set up — but it would mean adding a colour the app’s palette (styles.css) does not have, to say something orange already says on the build canvas. Agreeing with the canvas matters more here than agreeing with the pixel: they are both screens, read at the same distance, often minutes apart.

What actually moved because of this comparison. Not set up and No gate were red in the first draft. Lining them up against the pixel exposed that they aren’t faults at all — nothing is broken, the shop is unfinished — and the build canvas has always dotted an uncalibrated gate orange. They are orange now, which cost nothing and stopped two screens contradicting each other.

No blinking in the UI, and no blink codes anywhere. The pixel uses rate only as a second-order detail inside a colour, never as the primary signal — the header is blunt about why (counting flashes through sawdust at ten feet is miserable). The bin light’s red strobe is the same rule kept, not broken: red is what carries the meaning and the strobe only makes it carry further across a room. A chip has words, so it needs no second channel at all.

Dimmed, not hidden — and the subtitle still reports the power draw, so a tool that is being ignored while running says so rather than looking asleep.

Cost check: “In service” is new state, and the schema currently says it shouldn’t exist. The enabled flag in shared/device-model/shop.js is per port, not per machine, and RFC §6.6 makes the primary permanently enabled on purpose — the comment is explicit that “collect nothing from this tool” is what deleting the machine is for. So the Overarm guard switch above is free: it is exactly the existing enabled: false hood-off-the-saw case, which today has no UI at all. The In service switch is not — it needs a new machine-level field, routing that skips the machine, and a validateShop rule that stops it from being the reason a shop looks empty.

Worth doing anyway, I think: deleting a machine to sit out a repair loses its plug pairing, its gate calibration and its place on the canvas, and you rebuild all of it next week. But it is a schema change with a firmware side, not a UI tweak — so it is a separate change from the chips, landing after them.

The collector card, same axis

The card keeps its switch — unlike the tool rows, it is a real <button> already and it does what it looks like. What it gains is the chip, on the same one question rephrased for a blower: is this thing actually moving air?

The asymmetry that makes this possible: a tool’s plug senses, the collector’s plug commands. SmartOutletControl.h is explicit — collector slots are “switchable Shelly outlets (we turn them on/off) rather than power sensors”, and collectorOn(idx) returns what we commanded, never what is happening. So the card can be showing confident green while the blower’s own switch is off, the breaker has tripped, or nobody plugged it back in. The same Shelly reports watts — it is the identical ShellyGen2Outlet the tool slots poll — we simply never ask. Polling the collector plug for power was new firmware work — done 2026-08-24: reconcileCollectors() now polls each collector slot before it decides anything, SmartOutletControl stamps the spin-up clock at the command, and the runtime publishes systems[].plug alongside collectorOn. The verdict lives in collectorPlugState() in the shared model, not in the firmware — the device reports, the model judges, once.

Working. Green only when we commanded it on and the plug reports running current. That is a stronger claim than the card makes today.

Not starting The headline of the whole section. Relay closed, ~0 W on the other side of it. Its own switch is off, the breaker went, it’s unplugged, or the motor is stalled. Today you find this out by listening — which means the phone lies to you at exactly the moment you would most want to trust it.
Dead-headed Running with no path open. sequencer.js already computes deadHeadRisk, and it is the one hard rule this project has. If the state is ever reached the card should say so, not show green.
Blocked Orange, and this is where the tools’ “Waiting” went. A blower is never out-voted — it runs or it doesn’t — so there is no Waiting here. Blocked carries the same feeling: something wants air and isn’t getting it. The subtitle names both halves, because “which tool, and what’s stopping it” is the next question.
Bin full Red, to match the strobe already on the wall. A real discrete signal, not an inference — see the sensor note below. It is red rather than orange because you have to stop and deal with it: a full bin is lost suction, and past a point it is a fire and a clogged filter.
Coasting Grey, not green: the tools are already off and this is wind-down, not work. It is the one state the card gets right today, and it stays — a blower running with everything off otherwise reads as a stuck relay.

Spin-up is not a chip, it is a grace period. An induction motor takes a second or three to reach running draw, so Not starting evaluated the instant we close the relay would false-alarm on every single start. It is a debounce before raising the alarm, not a state to render — the same call we made about gate travel on the tool rows, for the same reason.

Filter loading is still not here. Nothing senses it, and the only available signals — a slowly rising baseline draw, accumulated runtime hours — are guesses dressed as measurements. Bin level is a different story entirely; see below.

A setup fact is a link, not a dead end

Two lines on this page name something that is not set up and then stop: “No outlet paired — there is nothing to switch” on a collector card, and “Manual · no outlet paired” under a tool. Both are correct, both are actionable, and until 2026-08-25 neither said where the action happens — you went looking for the screen that pairs an outlet. The collector was worse than that: its outlet could be paired nowhere but the build canvas, so the one thing the shop page could do nothing about was also the one thing it wouldn’t point you at.

An unpaired collector is orange where an unpaired tool is grey, and that asymmetry is deliberate. A tool with no outlet is one you hand-run from the shop list — that works, it is a whole legitimate way to own a machine, and nagging about it would be wrong. A blower with no outlet is one nothing on that page can switch on at all. Same words, different severity, and the two screens now say it in the same colour.

The lock had to be re-aimed. An unfinished layout greys this page out so nothing can drive hardware — and it greyed out the whole collector card and the whole tool list, which is where these links now live. Mid-setup is exactly when someone needs them, so the lock moved off the cards and onto the controls: the collector’s switch and the tool row’s button. A link switches nothing on. The red banner at the top of the page is what still says “none of this can run yet”, and it always was.

Suppressed — the state, kept; the switch, buried

“Don’t run this blower even if a tool asks.” Shop-vac days, and late evenings when the neighbours would rather not hear a 2 hp cyclone. It is a real errand — and a genuinely dangerous control to leave where a sleeve can catch it, because a suppressed collector looks exactly like a broken one to anyone who didn’t set it.

So the state gets a chip and the control gets buried. Those are separable, and splitting them is the whole design. Settings is where you go on purpose; the shop page is where you glance. Nothing on the shop page can turn suppression on, and the chip that says it is on is impossible to miss and one tap from the way out.

Grey, not orange. This was the close call. Orange would be truthful — a running saw is getting no air — but orange means something is wrong everywhere else on this page, and here nothing is: you asked for exactly this an hour ago. Grey plus a subtitle that names the cause and where to undo it says it without crying wolf.

Cost check. Cheaper than In service. It still needs schema, persistence and a firmware side; the only genuinely new rule is that gates must keep routing normally while it is on, so switching it back mid-cut can’t dead-head anything.

Bin and clog — two sources, both optional

There is already a Banner QS18VP6D diffuse photoelectric sensor watching the bin on the cyclone, driving a green / red-strobe light. It is discretely wired — no controller, because until now there was nothing for a controller to do with it. Bringing it into DustGate is planned and not designed yet.

It is not the only way in, and it must not become mandatory. A photoelectric sensor is not cheap, it is a nuisance on a shop vac that gets dragged around the shop, and plenty of people simply will not want one. So bin monitoring is per collector and has three settings — and the second one, watching the motor’s draw, is a seam left open rather than a feature being designed here.

Confidence sets the colour and the wording — this is the rule the whole page rests on. A discrete sensor gets red and “Bin full”, because we know. An inference gets orange and “Check suction”, because we don’t: it could be the bin, a clogged elbow, a blast gate someone left open, or a hose off a port. Wording it as an assertion would be the fastest way to make a woodworker stop believing every other chip on this page. Orange also already means no air here, and it isn’t the hardware’s fault, which is exactly the claim.
What it isWhat that buys, and what it costs
Discrete, not inferred This is the important one. A bin-level chip is not a guess from a drifting wattage baseline — it is a bit that is either set or not. It can be trusted the way the gate positions are trusted, which is why Bin full is a real chip above while filter loading is still in the not-built list.
Complementary PNP The part has both a normally-open and a normally-closed output. Reading both means a cut wire or a dead sensor is detectable — they stop disagreeing — instead of silently reading as “bin is fine forever”. That matters here more than it would elsewhere: a sensor that fails quiet is worse than no sensor, because you stop checking.
10–30 V dc, PNP The output swings to supply, and the C5 carrier is a 3.3 V part on a 5 V rail. It needs an optocoupler or a divider — this cannot land on a bare GPIO. A wiring question, not a UI one, but it is the reason this is “eventually” and not “next”.
Optional, per collector Cost, and a shop vac that moves. The status shape has to carry the source alongside the state, not just a boolean — that is what lets the card word itself honestly, and what stops a shop with no sensor inheriting a claim from one that has one. Cheap to get right now, awkward to retrofit.
It lives at the cyclone Which may well be a node, not the primary — and that is the real architectural question. Nodes today are dumb actuator banks: they receive already-resolved positions and send nothing back but pings. A sensor on a node makes it an input device, which is new NodeLink protocol, not new wiring. Worth deciding deliberately when the time comes rather than discovering it mid-build.

Per collector, and off by default. A picker rather than a switch — which source is in use is what the card’s wording depends on.

Watch the motor. Orange, a question, and it shows its working — the two numbers let you judge the guess instead of taking it on faith. It lists what it might be because it genuinely cannot tell.

Wattage clog detection: leave the seam, don’t cut the wood. No calibration, no thresholds, no learned baseline in this design — those need a real blower, a real bench and numbers nobody has measured. What this page fixes is the part that is expensive to change later: the chip exists, it is orange, it asks rather than asserts, and the status shape carries which source it came from. Adding the maths later is then a firmware change, not a redesign of this screen.

One physics note, unverified, so the first attempt doesn’t start backwards. The naive expectation is that a blockage makes the motor work harder. For a centrifugal blower or a vac it is the opposite — starve the inlet and mass flow drops, so the motor unloads and draw falls. It is the same effect as the pitch rising when you cover a shop-vac hose with your hand. So the signal to look for is running draw sagging below its learned baseline, not spiking above it. Nothing in this shop has measured it; treat it as the direction to test first, not a fact.

Does a full bin stop the shop? Not proposed here, and worth its own conversation — refusing to start a blower into a full bin is defensible, and so is “tell me, don’t decide for me”. This page only claims the chip. The stop-the-shop question touches routing and the never-dead-head rule and should not be settled as a side effect of a colour decision.
