Canvas · drag & drop

Dropping a piece in the seam should open a row for it

A shop with two collectors draws them as two grey grounds with a gap between. That gap is where a person aims when they want to add something — and it is the one place the canvas has no good answer for. Today it either swallows the gap or refuses the drop, depending on a distinction nobody can see.

1 · What happens today

Built — and this is the problem

A system owns a contiguous band of rows, and the two never interleave — that invariant is what makes the grey ground drawable at all (systemRowBands()). A drop is checked against the neighbour's band, not against the piece's own:

Where you drop itWhat happens now
In the gap, when there is a spare row between the two bands Allowed. The piece lands, and its own band grows to include that row. The gap is gone. The two grounds now meet.
In the gap, when the bands are already touching Refused — "That row belongs to Shop Vacuum. A piece stays in its own system."
Inside the neighbour's rows Refused, same message. Correct, and nothing below proposes changing it.

The first two rows of that table are the same gesture. Which one you get depends on whether a row happens to be spare, which is not drawn and cannot be counted by eye — so the canvas answers the same action two different ways and neither is what was asked for.

And it only works once (jeff, 2026-09-08): "the extra row vanishes once you do that, meaning that you can't keep moving things down." The two rows of that table are not two situations — they are the same situation before and after. The gap is a consumable: the first drop eats it, and from then on every drop in the seam is refused, because the bands now touch. There is no gesture that puts the row back, so the shop can only ever get tighter.

Half of this was already found and fixed — in the drawing. systemSeparators() exists because the empty row used to be the only thing separating two systems, and it "stopped the moment anything was dragged into it: two grounds that meet are one ground, and the drawing quietly claimed the shop had a single collector." A labelled rule was added so the seam survives being dragged shut. That kept the picture honest. It did not give the row back.

2 · The proposal — the seam is a row you can insert into

Proposed

Dropping a piece on the seam opens a new row there. Everything at or below that row moves down one — every system underneath, every piece in them, and every board — and the piece lands on the row that just opened. The seam survives: there is still a gap afterwards, because the shop grew rather than the band eating its neighbour's breathing room.

Which means it repeats. That is the whole test, and the thing today's behaviour fails: the seam is no longer something you spend. Drop into it as many times as you like — the demo below lets you — and it is still there afterwards, one row further down each time.

It is the instinct of inserting a row in a table, and it is the same operation whether or not a spare row happened to be there — which is what makes the two cases above stop disagreeing.

The shop as it stands. Cyclone owns rows 0–1, Shop Vacuum owns rows 3–4, and row 2 is the seam. Drum Sander is the piece being dragged.

The push is computed, not drawn. The scene above runs the actual rule — insertRowAt(r) bumps every piece and board with row >= r, then the bands are recomputed from where the pieces ended up, exactly as systemRowBands() does. If the picture looks right, the rule is right.

3 · What moves, and what does not

ThingDoes it move?Why
Every system below the seamDown one row Bands are contiguous and non-interleaving. Moving only the neighbour would close the next seam down instead of this one.
Every piece in themDown one row A band is not a container — it is derived from where its pieces stand. The pieces are the only thing there is to move.
BoardsDown one row, if at or below the seam A board belongs to no system but stands on a cell and owns it exclusively. Leaving it behind would drop it into a band it was never in, and put it in the way of the piece that just arrived.
Ducts and cablesReroute Nothing to move — they are derived from where the pieces are, and the router already re-solves on every change.
The system above the seamStays Insertion grows the shop downward. Pushing upward would move the piece you are still holding, which is the one thing a drop must not do.

4 · What still refuses, and why that is not an inconsistency

Dropping inside another system's rows stays refused. Inserting a row there would work mechanically — the shop would open up just the same — but the piece would then be standing on a row in the middle of the neighbour's band, which splits that band in two. That is precisely the interleaving the whole rule exists to prevent, and the grey ground would have no shape to draw. The seam is the only row where an insert leaves every band contiguous.

So the refusal message stays, but it is now rarer and more truthful: it means "that is inside another system", not "you happened to aim at a row that was already spoken for".

5 · Open — needs jeff's call before any of this is written

Open

Does the drag PREVIEW the push? The guide bar can say "Drop here and the shop opens a row" before the finger lifts, which is what every other drop does (dropWarn, at info). The alternative is showing it — sliding the lower systems down live while the piece hovers the seam. That is honest but it is a lot of movement for a gesture you might not complete, and this canvas has deliberately taken movement away before (D-68 dropped the whole-shop dimming for exactly that reason). Recommendation: say it, don't show it.

Is one row always enough? A unit that spans several columns still occupies one row, so yes for every piece the canvas can place today. Worth deciding out loud rather than discovering it later.

Does this need an undo? A drop that reflows twenty pieces is a bigger event than a drop that moves one. The canvas has undo/redo already; the question is whether the push is one undo step together with the drop, which it should be — but nothing checks that today.

Does the same gesture give a system breathing room on its own? The TODO calls this "the missing half" of no way to move a system. If dropping in the seam can insert a row, then dragging the seam itself — or a menu entry on the ground — could insert one with no piece involved. Out of scope here, but it is the same primitive and worth not designing against.

6 · What lands in canvas.html if this is accepted

One row of the What can stop a drag table is rewritten, and the table's opening claim — "only two things refuse a drop" — gets weaker in the right direction:

ConditionResult
WAS The row belongs to another system Refused. Systems own contiguous row bands; interleaving them makes the grey ground undrawable.
BECOMES The row is the seam between two systems Allowed — the shop opens a row. Everything at or below moves down one, so the seam survives instead of being swallowed.
STAYS The row is inside another system Refused. A piece there would split that band in two.
