ARCHIVED · decided against

Pinning the board rail

Do not build this. The board rail was deleted on 2026-08-16 and boards became ordinary pieces on the grid, so there is nothing to pin and no band to fade. This page is the record of what the rail was and why it looked reasonable at the time — it is not a proposal.

The reasoning that replaced it, and the alternatives rejected on the way, are in docs/boards-on-canvas-plan.md. It moved here out of secondary-ports.html, whose remaining panels — the second-pickup design, Option A — are still current.

The rail sits above the whole grid, so it leaves the screen the moment you scroll — and with it, which port drives which gate, and any hope of dragging a cable to a board you can't see. Stacking made that common, but it is not a multi-system problem: any shop taller than one screen has it.

Writing the board's name on each gate tab was the cheap answer and there isn't room for it. So: pin the rail, and give the band underneath it a soft fade — a strip the drawing passes under and the wires pass through, de-emphasised so the two don't fight.

At rest

scrolled to top

Nothing about the top of a shop should change. The fade only exists because of scrolling, so at scroll zero there is no fade and no pinning to notice.

Scrolled · pinned + fade

the pick
Whole wire, always

The rail sticks and slims — board bodies and their port strips, nothing else. Under it, a 60px gradient scrim fades the board out rather than cutting it off, so the drawing reads as passing beneath the rail instead of being clipped by it. Cables draw over the scrim, whole, because the one thing that must never be ambiguous is which port a gate is on.

Scrolled · hard edge

Reads as two panes

Pin it with a plain edge and no fade. Cheapest, and the cut is brutal: a gate bar sliding under the rail is severed mid-glyph. The fade costs one gradient and buys the sense that there is one continuous board.

How it works, and what it costs

Pinning is one translate. The rail is already drawn in the same SVG as the board, in negative y above the grid. Sticking it is translating that group down by the scroll offset divided by the zoom — no second coordinate system, no overlay, and the ports stay ordinary hit targets you can drop a cable on. Which is the whole reason not to reach for an HTML overlay.

Cables re-path, but they don't reshuffle. Every cable's start moves as you scroll, so the paths have to be recomputed — cheap, there are at most a handful. What must NOT be recomputed is the rank order, which is what stops two cables crossing: it is derived from horizontal travel, and travel changes with scroll. Freeze the ranks from the resting layout and let only the geometry stretch, and the lanes keep their order at every scroll position.

The scrim band is a free lane. Cables already pay a cost to cross a device (100) or a duct (3), which is what picks their drop column. Inside the faded band that cost goes to zero: the board under there is deliberately de-emphasised, so a wire crossing it says nothing misleading, and the alternative — cables detouring around glyphs that the user can barely see — would produce contortions with no visible cause.

The honest cost is clutter, which is why the pinned form is slimmer than the resting one. The network name and Find boards are errands you do once, at the top of the shop, so they stay behind at scroll zero; what sticks is the part you point at and drag to.
