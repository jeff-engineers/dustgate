Bus servo chains: ball valves, manifolds and sliders on bus servos

A third way to drive gates, beside hobby PWM servos: several ST3215 bus servos chained off one board, each reporting where it really is and how hard it is working. This page is for deciding the screens before any are built.

Decided 2026-11-01: one board per cluster of gates — a XIAO board with a 12 V jack, endstop inputs and up to 6 servos on one or two chains (one each side of the board, wire lengths still to measure), talking to the brain over WiFi like every node. The RS-485 trunk with drop boards was drawn first and dropped: a drop board needed its own chip anyway, so it might as well be the XIAO, and that removes the trunk, the bus board and a second protocol. ST3215 for now; the smaller STS3032 is noted as a good fit for later. The valve measured 2026-11-01 needs about 0.7 kg·cm, a fraction of what the ST3215 gives.
Beside PWM, not instead of it: the existing board stays as it is, for PWM gates and the collector. The bus board is a second board, and no board does both. CT clamp inputs on the bus board are a maybe for later.

On the shop layout

Drawn as it's wired · decided 2026-11-01

The cable is drawn the way the wire really runs: from one of the board's two chain ports to the nearest gate, then on to the next. A dot where the cable carries on is that servo's second socket. It's a drawing of a wire someone has to run, like the clamp lead, so a long one tells you to move the board, and the chain order is what the wire-length limit will be checked against once we've measured it.

Wiring a gate to it

Hold a gate's tab and drag it to a chain port, or onto the last gate of a chain to extend it. The board takes up to 6 servos across both chains. A 7th is refused with the reason: "This board drives 6 servos already. Put this gate on another bus board." The board still moves one servo at a time, like every board: they share its brick, and the wire-length limit counts on only the moving one drawing heavy current. A PWM gate dropped here, or a bus gate on a PWM board, is refused the same way the slider port is today.

A slider on a bus board

It sits on a chain like any servo and also uses the board's two endstop inputs, so a board takes one slider. Its tab says endstops under it, and a second slider is refused: "This board's endstops are used by the Planer slider."

The board picks the servo, not the drawing

Wiring a gate on the canvas says which board drives it. Which servo drives it is settled by Add a servo (plug it in, the board finds it), so the canvas never asks for a number. A gate wired on the canvas but with no servo yet shows its tab hollow, with "no servo yet" under it.

Open questions

  1. Decided 2026-11-01: "working harder than usual" is logged, not raised, until we have real numbers. Every move's peak load goes to the brain log and the servo's history; no warning, no amber. The threshold gets picked from shop data. A move that stalls (it stops short of where it was sent) is still red, as a failed move is today.
  2. Decided 2026-11-01: developer mode is a shop setting. One switch in Settings, saved on the brain, so every phone and browser in the shop sees the same thing.
  3. Decided 2026-11-01: one slider per bus board, so two endstop inputs. A second slider goes on another board.
  4. Decided 2026-11-01: servo numbers are shop-wide, and number 1 means "not yet assigned". The brain hands out 2–253, each used once in the shop and tied to its gate in the layout. A factory-fresh servo answers as 1, so it is always "new". A numbered servo that turns up on a different board can't clash: the app says whose servo it is and where it is now, and offers to keep it there. A servo with a number the layout doesn't know (from another project) is offered a reset to 1.
  5. The PWM pulse range moves into developer mode. With the new manifold inside 180° it should rarely be needed, and keeping it out of sight stops it being changed by accident.
